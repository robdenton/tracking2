This guide provides a categorized bank of open-ended questions grouped by goal: Operational blockers, manager support, retention risk, and strategic alignment. It also provides a framework for tracking qualitative trends across meeting cycles. Run skip-levels consistently with this structure, and you gain a ground-level view of organizational health that dashboards alone cannot provide.

Many skip-level meetings produce polite, safe answers. When you ask an employee two levels down "How are things going?", they often default to responses that protect their manager and hide the real bottlenecks1,2. The problem is not that employees withhold feedback maliciously. It is that the conditions for honest feedback are genuinely hard to create.

You already know you should run skip-levels. The harder questions are how to ask things that produce honest answers, and how to track those answers systematically enough to spot organizational patterns before they become crises. This guide provides a categorized question bank organized by intent, tactical advice on building trust in the room, and a framework for turning conversational notes into a searchable archive of organizational health.

Why skip-level meetings surface organizational blind spots

You make decisions based on information that travels up through layers of management. By the time feedback reaches you, it has been filtered, softened, and in some cases reversed entirely. Skip-level meetings short-circuit that process by giving you direct access to the people doing the work.

This is especially valuable when organizational alignment is at risk. Teams can be executing confidently on the wrong priorities, not because managers are hiding3 information, but because the translation of strategy into daily tasks is genuinely hard. Skip-levels surface those gaps before they become costly mistakes.

Gaining strategic skip-level insight

Communication gaps are a structural feature of hierarchical organizations, not a failure of individuals. These conversations help ensure messages are consistent between the employee, their manager, and the skip-level manager. Without that consistency check, teams can be working hard in the wrong direction for months before anyone notices. The strategic questions that surface this misalignment include:

  • "How does your day-to-day work connect to the company's current priorities?"
  • "What goals does your team own right now, and how were those set?"
  • "What parts of the company strategy feel unclear or distant from your daily work?"

How to build rapport in skip meetings

Psychological safety is the precondition for useful skip-level feedback. Without it, employees default to polished answers that say nothing actionable.

Yale IT's guidance on skip-level meetings recommends aiming for an 80/20 listen-to-talk ratio, where you listen intently and let the individual drive the conversation to the topics they want to discuss. In practice, this means asking one question and staying quiet long enough for the employee to think and respond. Filling silence, offering context, or defending a current process is the fastest way to shut down honest feedback.

How a leader opens the conversation determines whether the employee treats it as safe or formal. Leaders who establish upfront that the session is separate from performance evaluation and manager assessment tend to get more actionable feedback. The setup matters as much as the questions.

Probing for friction during skip-level chats

Operational friction is often invisible to you until it shows up as a missed deadline, a failed launch, or an unexpected resignation.4,5 Skip-level meetings surface friction before it accumulates to that point.

Pinpointing operational and efficiency blockers

Bottleneck questions work best when they are specific and forward-looking rather than general and retrospective. Staying curious throughout is the discipline that matters most here: When someone shares something surprising or uncomfortable, respond with a follow-up question rather than context or justification.

Effective bottleneck questions include:

  • "What's taking up most of your time right now, and does that feel like the right use of it?"
  • "If you could remove one obstacle from your workflow tomorrow, what would it be?"
  • "Where do handoffs between team members create unnecessary delays or confusion?"
  • "What's one thing we do as a team that we should stop doing immediately?"
  • "Is there information you need to do your job well that you don't currently have access to?"

Administrative overhead is often the silent tax on team output.6,7 Employees spending time on status updates, approvals, or redundant reporting rarely flag this proactively because they assume it's part of the job. These questions bring it to the surface.

Identifying cross-functional dependency gaps

Cross-functional alignment is one of the most common sources of hidden friction in scaling organizations.8,9 Cross-functional gaps are a common source of hidden10 friction because no single team owns the handoff.

The questions that follow do not need the ranking to justify them

Questions that surface these gaps include:

  • "How well do you feel the engineering and product teams are aligned on our current roadmap priorities?"
  • "Which cross-functional handoffs feel the most broken right now?"
  • "What assumptions are we making about our users that you think might be incorrect?"
  • "Is there information another team holds that would help you do your job better, but that you don't currently have access to?"

For teams where product and engineering share a roadmap, these questions also surface whether the underlying assumptions are shared across the full delivery chain. The same probing approach used in discovery call documentation transfers directly here: Probe for the assumptions behind current decisions, not just the decisions themselves.

Capturing friction patterns across sessions

The diagnostic value of these questions compounds over time, but only if you capture and track the answers systematically.

Evaluating manager support during skip-level talks

Skip-levels give you a rare view into the quality of management one level below you. This is valuable information, but it requires careful handling. Focus your questions on the employee's experience and growth, not on the manager's behavior directly. That distinction shifts the conversation from judgment to development.

Assessing mentorship and feedback quality

Professional development questions reveal whether employees feel actively supported or left to figure things out independently. Paradigmie's guide to skip-level questions notes that uneven access to mentoring and high-visibility work is one of the most common sources of quiet frustration in scaling teams, with remote employees often at a particular disadvantage.

Questions for assessing mentorship and feedback quality include:

  • "How does your manager support your professional development goals?"
  • "When was the last time you received feedback that genuinely helped you improve your work?"
  • "Do you feel like you know what you'd need to do to advance in your current role?"
  • "When you've had a challenging project, how supported did you feel in working through it?"

Feedback quality is one of the strongest predictors of team development. Employees who receive clear, specific feedback improve faster and feel more engaged. Those who receive vague or infrequent feedback often describe feeling stuck or uncertain about their standing.

How to clarify team decision making

Ambiguity about who owns a decision is a primary cause of delay, and Deloitte's research notes it also raises the risk that decisions fall through the cracks entirely. Skip-levels are well-positioned to assess whether decision-making processes are working at the team level.

Questions that probe decision-making include:

  • "When a decision needs to be made on your team, is it clear who has the final say?"
  • "Can you think of a recent decision that took longer than it should have? What slowed it down?"
  • "Are there decisions you're currently making that you wish had clearer guidelines or more authority behind them?"

Detecting turnover risks in skip-level sessions

Employees rarely resign without warning. The signals are typically visible in qualitative conversations weeks or months before a resignation letter arrives. Skip-levels are one of the most effective early-warning systems available to you, but only if the questions create space for honest answers about morale, workload, and future outlook.

Identifying early disengagement and turnover risk

General morale questions work best when they invite employees to speak about the team's collective experience rather than their own. This framing reduces defensiveness and often produces more candid answers because employees feel more comfortable describing what they observe in peers than what they feel personally.

Questions in this category include:

  • "How do you feel about the overall morale of the team right now?"
  • "How excited are you about the work your team is doing, compared to six months ago?"
  • "Do you feel like your contributions are recognized and valued by the organization?"
  • "When you think about the next year at this company, what do you feel most uncertain about?"

How to detect hidden retention risks11,12

Career trajectory questions reveal whether employees see a future at the company. Those who cannot articulate a path forward are at significantly higher retention risk, even if they appear satisfied in the short term.

Questions in this area include:

  • "What opportunities do you see for yourself to grow within this company over the next year?"
  • "Are there skills you want to develop that you don't currently have an opportunity to work on here?"
  • "What would need to change for you to feel more excited about your future here?"

How to track trends between skip-levels

Running a single skip-level produces useful qualitative data. Running skip-levels consistently over time and synthesizing the patterns across them produces organizational intelligence. The gap between those two outcomes is almost entirely a documentation problem.

Most leaders take notes in notebooks or scattered files. The insights from one session are rarely connected to the insights from the next. When you ask "Are these engineering team frustrations getting better or worse?", nobody can answer with confidence because the data was never organized to support that question.

FAQs

What is a skip-level meeting?

A skip-level meeting is a one-on-one conversation between a senior leader and an employee who reports to one of that leader's direct reports, bypassing the direct manager in the conversation. The goal is to give leadership direct access to front-line feedback without the filtering that naturally occurs as information travels up through management layers.

How many questions should you ask in a skip-level meeting?

Two to three anchor questions per session works well for a 45-minute meeting. Yale IT's guidance recommends an 80/20 listen-to-talk ratio, which leaves little room for a long question list. Asking more produces a checklist conversation rather than a diagnostic one.

Link text → https://its.yale.edu/about-it/it-staff-resources/performance-management/engaging-our-teams-through-skip-level-meetingsYale IT's guidance
Link URLhttps://its.yale.edu/about-it/it-staff-resources/performance-management/engaging-our-teams-through-skip-level-meetings

How do you prevent skip-level meetings from feeling like interrogations?

Send a brief agenda with sample questions two or three days in advance, open with an explicit statement that the meeting is not a performance review, and maintain the 80/20 listening ratio throughout.

Should you tell direct managers you're running skip-levels?

Yes. Informing direct managers that skip-levels are happening and explaining why is the right starting point, so there are no surprises. Transparency with managers about the process is what distinguishes skip-levels from surveillance.23

Can you use an AI tool to capture skip-level meetings without disrupting the conversation?

Yes. Glossary

80/20 listen-to-talk ratio: A guideline for skip-level facilitation, recommended by Yale IT, in which the leader spends approximately 80% of the meeting listening and 20% asking questions or providing brief context. The ratio reinforces that the meeting exists to surface the employee's perspective, not to share the leader's.

Cross-functional alignment: The degree to which teams across different functions (engineering, product, sales, etc.) share a consistent understanding of priorities, ownership, and handoff expectations. Misalignment here is one of the most common sources of duplicated effort and missed deadlines in scaling organizations.

Folder-level query: A capability in Granola that lets you ask questions across all meeting notes stored in a specific folder, returning pattern-matched answers with inline citations linking back to individual sessions. Used in skip-level workflows to surface recurring themes across multiple conversation cycles.

Operational friction: Any process, approval step, or workflow pattern that slows down an employee's ability to do their core work. Skip-level questions are designed to surface friction that employees normalize and stop reporting through regular channels.

Organizational blind spot: Information about team health, morale, or execution problems that exists at the front-line level but does not travel accurately up through management layers. Skip-level meetings are a structured method for closing that gap.

Psychological safety: The shared belief within a team or conversation that it is safe to speak up, disagree, or share difficult feedback without fear of punishment or judgment. It is the precondition for any skip-level meeting producing honest, actionable answers.

Retention risk: The likelihood that an employee will leave the organization, often detectable weeks or months before a formal resignation through qualitative signals in conversation: uncertainty about career trajectory, declining engagement, or unresolved frustrations.

Skip-level meeting: A one-on-one conversation between a senior leader and an employee who reports to one of that leader's direct reports, conducted without the direct manager present. The purpose is to give leadership unfiltered access to front-line feedback.
